5  Simulating a Human 2.0 map

NoteA worked example of the map, not a patient result

The first map is a breast with a 2 cm tumour. Two million photons is what the real instrument collects in 1 second, so each step of the map is 1 s of beam time. This breast was stepped at 5 mm, and only where it is 2 cm or thicker: 166 steps, which is 166 seconds (2 min 46 s). A dense breast with a 2 cm tumour follows. The comparison with each breast once the tumour is removed is at the end.

The simulator builds a 3D breast from adipose and a branching glandular network, and can add a tumour of any chosen size. The volume used here is a half breast: semi-axes 5.5 cm in x and y, height 5.5 cm, voxels of 5 mm, on a 24 × 12 × 12 grid at 0.5 cm (Figure 5.1). Materials are assigned per voxel (adipose, then glandular).

A run has two stages. First the photons interact in the breast and are stored. Then they are passed to a detector. The detector and the sample-to-detector distance can be changed after the breast has been simulated, without repeating it. The detector here is the CdTe Dectris 500K that Human 2.0 will move to.

Figure 5.2 is one such run, 2 million photons, aimed at a very fatty part of this breast. About 16% of the beam is transmitted, and the 1D curve has the fat peak near q = 14 nm⁻¹ and little water, the same shape as the fatty scans in the real data.

5.1 A map of every thick point

The same 2-million-photon run was repeated on the UCL cluster at every point of this breast that is 2 cm or thicker. That is the scan proposed for Human 2.0 (Chapter 4): leave out the thin edge, and give each remaining point one second. At 5 mm steps that is 166 points, so 2 min 46 s of beam time, inside the 5-minute limit.

This map is a breast with a 2 cm tumour (Figure 5.3). Each simulated point was prepared exactly as a real scan: the fat peak was shifted to q = 14 nm⁻¹, and the curve was normalised the same way. Thickness and transmission give the linear attenuation, μ = −ln(T) / thickness. A 3 × 3 kernel, nine neighbouring points, was slid over every position, and the search keeps the highest Water:Fat automatically.

Figure 5.4 puts the two readings of this breast side by side. On the left, linear attenuation behaves like a mammogram: glandular tissue and the tumour are both dense, so both are bright. On the right, the 3 × 3 kernel map stays low over the rest of the breast, and the red box is the kernel the search kept. It sits on the tumour.

5.2 A dense breast, with a 2 cm tumour

Figure 5.5 is a dense breast: glandular tissue fills much more of the slice, and a 2 cm tumour sits in it. Figure 5.6 is the same pair of readings as Figure 5.4. With more glandular tissue, linear attenuation shows one broad dense region, so the tumour is harder to separate from the tissue around it. The 3 × 3 search still outlines it. The red box is that kernel, Water:Fat 0.337, on the tumour.

5.3 Compared with no tumour

The same 3 × 3 search, on the same breast with the tumour removed and nothing else changed. With a tumour the summed curve rises through the water band. Without one it stays with the global mean, and the breast would fall on the fatty side of the top 15% cut (Chapter 1) and of the water-peak rule at 0.35 (Appendix C, Figure F.5).

5.3.1 The less dense breast

Figure 5.7 is the less dense breast with the 2 cm tumour. The kernel the search keeps has Water:Fat 0.292. Figure 5.8 is that breast with the tumour removed. The wettest kernel is then 0.176, and the red curve lies on the global mean through the water band.

5.3.2 The dense breast

Figure 5.9 is the dense breast with the 2 cm tumour. The kernel the search keeps has Water:Fat 0.337. Figure 5.10 is that breast with the tumour removed. The wettest kernel is then 0.192, and the red curve stays with the global mean.
